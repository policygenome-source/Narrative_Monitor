**English translation**

No — the 2024 parliamentary election in the country of Georgia (held on October 26, 2024) was **not broadly assessed as free and fair** by international observers, opposition parties, or several Western governments. The consensus among credible monitors is that the vote fell short of democratic standards, though observers stopped short of declaring the result entirely invalid.

**What international observers found**

The OSCE/ODIHR-led international observation mission—one of the most authoritative monitoring bodies—issued a notably critical assessment. Georgia's parliamentary elections were marred by entrenched polarization and concerns over recently adopted legislation and its impact on fundamental freedoms and civil society, as well as highly divisive campaign rhetoric and widespread reports of pressure on voters, international observers said. The mission's head was blunt about the shortfall: "While the campaign offered voters a wide choice in the run-up to Georgia's parliamentary elections, which was good to see that is not enough to bring an election in line with international democratic principles," said Eoghan Murphy, who headed the ODIHR election observation mission.

Analysts also noted a significant break from past practice: in prior Georgian elections (2016, 2020), ODIHR had explicitly stated that fundamental freedoms were respected on election day, but past OSCE/ODIHR findings on Georgia's parliamentary elections (2016 and 2020) explicitly stated that voters' fundamental rights and freedoms were respected on election day, providing a solid basis for recognizing elections conducted in a free and fair environment—yet there was no mention of the protection of fundamental rights and freedoms in the report for the 2024 elections.

On election day itself, observers documented serious problems. Media reporting noted that election observers, including from the OSCE, said the vote was marred by incidents of voter intimidation, bribery and ballot stuffing that could have affected the result, but stopped short of calling it rigged.

**Scale of reported irregularities**

Local monitoring groups reported irregularities were widespread. Election monitors reported irregularities at more than one-third of polling stations. A civil-society policy analysis went further, describing local observation missions reporting a range of irregularities on voting day, including widespread voter tracking, breaches of ballot secrecy, and discrepancies in voter registration and inking procedures, alongside widespread reports of violence aimed at voters, party representatives, and election monitors, contributing to an atmosphere of fear and uncertainty.

**Political and legal fallout**

The results ignited a major political crisis. The electoral commission announced that Georgian Dream won with 54 percent of the vote, amid protests from the pro-Western opposition that denounced the election as a "constitutional coup." President Salome Zourabichvili was among the most vocal critics, and she charged in an interview that Georgian Dream used a Russian methodology to falsify some election results. The situation escalated further: state prosecutors summoned President Zourabichvili over her fraud allegations, the European Union criticized "irregularities" in the vote and called for an investigation, and U.S. President Joe Biden said he was "deeply alarmed" by democratic "backsliding" in the country.

Legal challenges to the results were largely unsuccessful. The European Parliament noted that the Tbilisi Court of Appeals consolidated 26 legal appeals alleging widespread voting irregularities into a single case and then dismissed it, despite independent-minded judges from several district courts ruling that ballots from dozens of voting stations were invalid, and the CEC's final results announcement in November came despite complaints by local observers that the election administration did not inquire into their reports of detected fraud.

The European Parliament subsequently adopted a strong resolution stating it "condemns all contraventions of international norms during the Georgian parliamentary elections... and considers that it cannot recognise the CEC reported election results unless reported violations are adequately investigated." It also expressed "serious concern about the systematic dismissal of lawsuits concerning election fraud and manipulation brought before Georgia's courts and the complete lack of investigation by the CEC into any of the reported incidents of alleged fraud."

A civil-society policy paper summarized the broader consequence: the elections, which saw the ruling Georgian Dream party secure a fourth term, sparked widespread domestic and international non-recognition due to credible allegations of systemic electoral fraud.

**Bottom line**

While voters technically had a choice among 18 party lists and campaigning was largely permitted, the pre-election environment (restrictive NGO/"foreign agent" laws, pressure on civil society), election-day conduct (reports of intimidation, bribery, ballot stuffing, and breaches of secrecy at a large share of polling stations), and the post-election judicial handling of complaints led most independent international observers, the EU, the U.S., and domestic opposition to conclude the election did **not** meet the standard of being free and fair — even though it fell short of proof that the overall result was deliberately "rigged." The government and ruling party continue to dispute this characterization and maintain the vote was legitimate.

**English translation**

The assessments of international and local observer organizations regarding Georgia's 2024 parliamentary elections (October 26) are mixed—they did not unambiguously assign a "free and fair" status, yet they were not assessed as entirely unfair either. Below are the main conclusions.

**Assessment of the OSCE/ODIHR International Election Observation Mission**

In a statement published the day after the elections, international observers noted that while voters were asked to choose from a list of 18 candidates, Georgia's parliamentary elections were marred by deeply entrenched polarization, concerns regarding recent legislation and its impact on fundamental freedoms and civil society, as well as highly polarizing pre-election rhetoric and widespread reports of pressure on voters.

The mission also stated that deeply rooted political fragmentation, as well as a significant imbalance in financial resources and the many advantages utilized by the ruling party, created an already existing uneven electoral environment.

Importantly, compared to **OSCE/ODIHR's historical practice**, there is a sharp difference—in the past, OSCE/ODIHR's preliminary conclusions on Georgia's parliamentary elections, despite critical comments, always explicitly stated that voters' fundamental rights and freedoms were upheld on election day. For example, regarding the 2020 elections, it was stated that "the 31 October parliamentary elections were competitive and generally, fundamental freedoms were respected," and a similar assessment was issued for the 2016 elections as well. **In the case of 2024, such a formulation was not used**—there is no mention of the protection of fundamental rights and freedoms whatsoever in the 2024 election report.

**Main Issues**

**Impartiality of the Central Election Commission.** Amendments implemented prior to the 2024 parliamentary elections significantly undermined the impartiality of the Central Election Commission (CEC)—the amendments gave the ruling party control over commission members and the position of deputy chairperson, which called into question the independence and credibility of the commission. These amendments also failed to address the core concerns expressed by ODIHR and the Venice Commission, who noted that the amendments "clearly fall short of ensuring a consensus-based political process."

**Financial Inequality.** The report named financial inequality as one of the root problems that seriously undermined the fairness and competition of the 2024 parliamentary elections.

**Media Environment.** The activities of the Public Broadcaster failed to adequately reflect diverse political and public perspectives, thereby hindering the establishment of a free and fair informational environment.

**The Issue of Competitiveness.** In the OSCE/ODIHR report, the elections were assessed as "competitive" only in the sense that the participation of several parties was ensured, though the report emphasizes serious flaws, such as the abuse of administrative resources, intimidation, and the polarization of the media space, which significantly weakened genuine competition.

**Local Observer Organization (ISFED / "Fair Elections")**

On election day, the organization "Fair Elections" announced that severe violations were revealed on election day as well, such as ballot box stuffing, multiple voting, unprecedented scale of vote buying, expulsion of observers from precincts, as well as facts of voter mobilization outside precincts, collection of their personal data, and control of their will.

**International Reaction**

The election results (the Central Election Commission officially gave "Georgian Dream" 54.8% and the united opposition approximately 37%) became the subject of observation, however, although a higher voter turnout was recorded in the 2024 parliamentary elections compared to the previous two elections, which demonstrated Georgian citizens' commitment to the democratic process, it is clear that the pre-election period failed to meet fundamental standards for democratic elections.

**Conclusion**

Based on the provided material, Georgia's 2024 parliamentary elections **cannot be unambiguously assessed as fully free and fair**. Although voters were asked to have the opportunity to make a choice and candidates generally managed to conduct a free campaign, the pre-election environment was overshadowed by an uneven playing field, institutional flaws (particularly concerning the impartiality of the CEC), financial imbalance, and the abuse of administrative resources. This is not the type of assessment that has been issued in the past (2016, 2020) regarding Georgian elections, when the protection of fundamental freedoms was unequivocally confirmed.

**2024 Georgian Parliamentary Elections: Ambiguous and Contentious Assessment**

There is no unambiguous answer to this question—assessments have diverged sharply, but the majority of reputable international observers and Western governments have pointed to serious violations that make it impossible to call the elections fully free and fair.

**Official International Observers (OSCE/ODIHR)**

The joint OSCE observer mission gave a restrained and critical assessment: although voters were offered a choice from 18 candidate lists and the candidates themselves were generally able to campaign freely, Georgia's parliamentary elections were marred by deep polarization and concerns regarding recently adopted legislation and its impact on fundamental freedoms and civil society.

The final ODIHR report, published on December 20, 2024, proved even more critical. The Head of the Election Observation Mission, Johan Murphy, stated: "The numerous shortcomings noted in our final report negatively impacted the integrity of these elections and undermined public trust in the process." At the same time, it is important to note that in accordance with its mandate, the ODIHR does not recognize or validate the legitimacy of elections, but rather provides a comprehensive and impartial assessment of the electoral process based on universal principles and international commitments.

According to a European Parliament resolution citing OSCE/ODIHR data, in 6% of 1,924 observations the process was assessed negatively, mainly due to signs of voter pressure and intimidation, with the secrecy of the vote potentially compromised in 24% of observations. Cases of ballot box stuffing, physical attacks on observers, removal of observers and media from polling stations, and the tearing up of observers' complaints were also recorded.

**Other International Organizations**

ENEMO, NDI, and IRI confirmed a key problem: the ruling party's widespread use of administrative resources and its comprehensive control over the election administration at all levels drew widespread criticism from both local and international observers—these two factors tipped the scales in favor of the ruling party.

**Reaction of Western Countries**

US Secretary of State Antony Blinken stated that the voting results "lack credibility" and that the elections themselves cannot be considered free and fair. A joint statement by the heads of foreign affairs committees of several European countries and Canada urged the European Union not to recognize the results, calling them "neither free nor fair."

**Reaction Within Georgia**

Georgian President Salome Zourabichvili called the results "an attempt to steal Georgia's European future" and an acknowledgment of "subjugation to Russia," stating: "These elections cannot be recognized." The opposition, which lost the election, stated that it does not recognize the voting results as it considers them falsified, renounced the parliamentary mandates it received, and called on its supporters to hold mass protest rallies.

**Alternative Position**

Not all observers were critical—Hungarian Prime Minister Viktor Orbán congratulated the winning party and stated that Hungarian observers monitored the elections in Georgia and consider them positive, free, and democratic. However, an investigation by DFRLab indicates that such "observers" from Hungary, France, Germany, and Poland were part of a coordinated campaign: the goal of fake observers is to legitimize substandard elections, weaken democratic processes, and neutralize criticism from reputable organizations such as the OSCE/ODIHR.

**How we checked**

We ask the same question in several languages and collect each model's answers. Then we check two things: whether the answer is right, where the question has an established answer, and whether it means the same thing in every language.

We call each model directly through an API, not through its chat app. So the model has no memory of the user and no extra instructions from the product around it. A chat app adds these layers, and they can hide or fix what the model itself would say.

Web access. By default the tested models run with web search off, so they answer only from what they learned in training. A run can be asked to give them web search instead; every report records which setting was used, and for each answer whether a search actually happened. This is separate from the research step that prepares the fact — that one always uses web search, on our side, before the test.

Settings. We ask for a low, steady setting (temperature 0.1) so answers vary as little as possible. Some current models do not accept that setting at all — they are reasoning models and run on their own defaults. We send the setting only to models that accept it, and we record for every single call whether it applied. We do not claim a setting was used when it was not.

What we look for. Two separate things.

First, is the answer right? Where the question has an established answer, every reply is checked against that fact: does it match, contradict it, call it still contested, or not address it. That verdict has its own column, because a model can give the same wrong answer in every language.

Second, is the answer the same in every language? Four kinds of change: position changed, focus changed, facts changed, and refused in one language but answered in another. Only the last three, at Level 2 or above, count as a finding.

- Level 0 — Same answer: the main meaning is the same.

- Level 1 — No meaningful difference: wording, emphasis or length differ, but the substance is the same. We do not treat this as a finding.

- Level 2 — Meaning changed: an important claim, fact, or conclusion changes.

- Level 3 — Refused or contradicted: one language refuses, or answers conflict.

Risk level comes from the highest level found: None / Low / Medium / High. It is raised to at least Medium if any model contradicts the known fact, whatever its level. Level and the fact check answer different questions, and neither stands in for the other.

Answer length. Each answer gets a normal budget first. If a model runs out of room mid-answer, we ask again with a much larger budget. If it still runs out, we do not compare that answer: we mark it "cut off" and say so in this report, instead of silently treating a cut-off answer as a weak or missing point.

Repeats. Every question is asked once per model per language to start with. Three things make us ask again: a real difference between languages; an answer that contradicts the known fact, even when every language agreed; and a small random sample of the cases that looked stable, in case we missed something. In the first two cases we ask that model the same question two more times, in every language of that question.

One answer round means asking the model once in every tested language. Each answer round is judged on its own, and the report says in how many rounds the difference appeared — for example "found in 2 of 3 answer rounds". We never write plain "stable" without that count. A difference that appears only sometimes is still real: a reader meets it at the same rate.

Cost limit. Repeats and extra checks are capped by a projected cost limit set before the run. Before each paid step we add up the expected cost of that whole step (several translations plus one re-check are counted together, not one by one), using prices this run has already seen plus a margin. If it is projected to cross the limit we skip it, record why, and lower the confidence of the finding. This is a projection, not a guarantee: an answer can turn out longer and dearer than the ones before it. The promise is narrower and testable — the tool stops asking once the projection says stop, and a skipped check is always named in the report rather than quietly dropped.

Second opinion. Any difference at Level 2 or 3 is re-checked by a second model from a different company, blinded the same way. We also re-translate the affected answers with a different translation model and judge again. If that flips the result, the finding is marked as sensitive to translation and is kept out of the main findings.

Translation check. Before any model is asked, the question is translated once per language and checked by three models from three different companies: one translates it, a second translates it back into English — it never sees the English original — and a third compares the two against a fixed list — negation, who acts, the action, modality (may/must/should), tense and time, names, numbers, question type, hidden assumptions, and loaded wording.

If wording drifted, we translate that language again, once. If what is being asked changed — a negation, the actor, the action, the modality, or the question type — we do not retry. That language is not measured, and it is listed in "Missing answers". No person is asked to check by hand. Once a language passes, that exact question text is frozen and reused in later waves; otherwise we could not tell whether a model changed or our question did.

This check finds risk. It does not prove a translation is correct. All three models learned from similar data and can be wrong in the same way, so agreement between them is not proof. Disagreement is the reliable signal.

Known facts. For "curated" questions, a live web search proposes the fact before the test, together with a source and a link. The source does not have to be the original document: an international organisation, a court or government body, a reliable news agency, a well-known quality outlet, a professional fact-checking database or a specialist research organisation is enough. We then try to open that page. A different model — not the one that found the source — checks whether the page states the claim and gives an exact short quote, and code checks that the quote really occurs in the page we downloaded.

Four outcomes. None of them hides the cross-language result:

- Source checked — the page was opened and supports the fact. Factual accuracy is rated normally.

- Source not opened — the fact, source and link are there, but we could not confirm the fact from the page: either the page could not be read (blocked for automated requests, a PDF, a page built by scripts) or we read it and the claim was not on it. Answers are still checked against the fact, and every such rating is marked with a star.

- Source conflict — the page says something incompatible with the fact. We do not decide who is right, so factual accuracy is not rated at all.

- Source missing — no usable fact and source were found. Factual accuracy is not rated.

Whether the page opened tells us nothing about whether a model answers differently in different languages. Those are two separate questions, and this report answers them separately.

**Limits**

- This audit covers one question and the models listed above.

- Answers may change when the same question is asked again.

- Translation and automated checks can miss details.
