**Short answer: No — but the issue is currently contested**

Under longstanding federal law, ICE agents (and other armed federal officers or troops) are **not legally permitted** to be stationed at or sent to polling places. However, this has become a live legal controversy in 2026, with the Trump administration signaling it may deploy ICE to polls under certain circumstances, prompting lawsuits and state-level countermeasures.

**The legal basis**

A Civil War–era federal statute is the core protection here: a federal law dating to the end of the Civil War already bans sending the military or other "armed men" to polling places, except to repel armed enemies of the United States. More specifically, under federal law, an official who orders, brings or keeps "troops or armed men" at a location where a general or special election is taking place can face up to five years in prison and disqualification from federal office.

This is codified in specific statutes: voter intimidation — including by federal, state, and local officials — is illegal under federal laws such as 18 U.S.C. § 592, prohibiting federal officials from deploying "armed men at any place where a general or special election is held."

Legal experts note the scope is broad — the Brennan Center for Justice has interpreted the statute broadly to cover all locations where votes are cast and counted, including polling places, ballot tabulation sites, and drop boxes, and under that reading, there is no version of an ICE presence at or near an election site that passes legal muster. ICE's actual legal mandate reinforces this: ICE's statutory mandate is immigration enforcement, both civil and criminal, deriving from the Homeland Security Act and the Immigration and Nationality Act — neither of which grants ICE any role in election administration, voter identity verification, or poll security.

**Why this is controversial right now**

Despite the clear legal prohibition, senior Trump administration officials have refused to rule out ICE presence at polls ahead of the November 2026 midterms. DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin stated "the only reason why we would be at polling places is if there is a threat to that polling place or we're serving a warrant on someone that we have been actively tracking down... If we're serving a warrant we will be where we need to be." President Trump himself said he would "do anything necessary to make sure we have honest elections; we have to have honest elections."

This has already led to real-world incidents: during primary elections, there were also multiple reports in various states of federal law enforcement activity inside or near polling sites, including in Texas and California, where local law enforcement intervened.

**Legal pushback**

Civil rights groups have filed multiple lawsuits. One argues that the policy violates the federal law that prohibits "armed men" at election sites, and also causes voter intimidation, which is barred under the Voting Rights Act. Additionally, earlier this year, senior administration officials publicly questioned why ICE officers should not be sent to polling stations, the U.S. Department of Justice removed longstanding guidance explaining restrictions on federal law enforcement activity at polling places, and ICE agents conducted enforcement actions at active polling sites in Texas and New York.

Several states are also acting proactively — measures to restrict federal agents from operating at or near election-related locations have been offered in more than half a dozen states.

**Bottom line**

Legally, no — ICE has no authorized role at polling places, and armed federal agents are explicitly banned by federal law except to repel an actual armed attack on the U.S. That said, administration officials have suggested they might send agents anyway under a "warrant" justification, and this is currently being fought out in courts and state legislatures ahead of the 2026 midterms. If ICE does appear at a polling site, that would be a violation of federal law, and voters/election officials have legal recourse (courts have already gotten involved to block such deployments).

**English translation**

**Can ICE agents legally be present at polling places in the United States?**

No, under current U.S. federal law, the presence of armed federal agents, including ICE personnel, at polling places is illegal. This is confirmed by multiple legal experts and organizations.

**Legal Basis of the Prohibition**

The primary legislative act is 18 U.S.C. § 592, which states that any federal official who orders, brings, or keeps "troops or armed men" at a place where a general or special election is held can face up to five years in prison and be disqualified from holding federal office.

This law, enacted in 1865, contains only a narrow exception: the law contains only a narrow exception when such force is necessary to "repel armed enemies of the United States."

In addition, there is another precedent from the practice of enforcing the law: the Department of Justice's own longstanding guidelines on election crimes have interpreted this prohibition to mean that FBI agents cannot conduct investigations inside polling places on Election Day, and U.S. Marshals cannot be stationed at open polling locations because they are armed while on duty. DOJ guidance has similarly indicated that federal prosecutors have "no authority to direct FBI special agents or U.S. Marshals to polling places."

**Additional Legal Protection**

Beyond this direct prohibition, there is protection against voter intimidation: anyone intimidated by ICE agents or military personnel can ask a judge to block such intimidation under the Voting Rights Act of 1965; the law also protects individuals "urging or helping others to vote" from intimidation. Moreover, under this law, a plaintiff only needs to prove that they were intimidated, rather than that the intimidating party had such an intent.

It is also important to note: many states also prohibit carrying weapons at or near polling places, though these exemptions often apply to local law enforcement, but do not always exempt federal law enforcement or military personnel from them.

**Current Political Situation (2026)**

Despite the unambiguous nature of the law, in 2026 this topic became the subject of sharp political debate in connection with the upcoming midterm elections:

- Secretary of Homeland Security **Markwayne Mullin** stated that ICE agents could be present at polling places if they are executing an immigration warrant.

- In response, Maine Secretary of State Shenna Bellows emphasized: "The Constitution and federal law prohibit ICE agents from being at polling places"... "As Secretary of State and Maine’s chief election official, we will not tolerate this lawless and reckless proposal."

- The situation escalated to the point where a coalition of civil rights groups and labor unions filed a lawsuit against the Trump administration to prevent the deployment of ICE agents at polling places during the November midterm elections—marking the second such lawsuit this month, following statements by high-ranking administration officials regarding federal policy allowing ICE agents to enter polling locations under certain circumstances to conduct immigration enforcement.

- It was also reported that the Department of Justice removed longstanding guidelines clarifying restrictions on federal law enforcement activities at polling places, and that ICE agents had conducted enforcement actions at active polling locations in Texas and New York.

**Practical Aspects**

Experts note that a mass presence of ICE at all polling places is physically unrealistic: in 2024, there were 95,815 polling places in the U.S., whereas ICE, even accounting for a recent 120-percent surge in hiring, has around 22,000 agents—if the agency attempted to station a single agent at each polling place, it would cover only about a quarter of them.

**Conclusion**

Formally, the law is unambiguous: the deployment of armed federal agents, including ICE, at polling places or anywhere else where elections are held, **is a violation of federal law**. However, Trump administration officials have repeatedly refused to categorically rule out the possibility of such deployment, which has led to lawsuits and preparations by local authorities for potential unlawful actions ahead of the 2026 midterm elections.

**How we checked**

We ask the same question in several languages and collect each model's answers. Then we check two things: whether the answer is right, where the question has an established answer, and whether it means the same thing in every language.

We call each model directly through an API, not through its chat app. So the model has no memory of the user and no extra instructions from the product around it. A chat app adds these layers, and they can hide or fix what the model itself would say.

Web access. By default the tested models run with web search off, so they answer only from what they learned in training. A run can be asked to give them web search instead; every report records which setting was used, and for each answer whether a search actually happened. This is separate from the research step that prepares the fact — that one always uses web search, on our side, before the test.

Settings. We ask for a low, steady setting (temperature 0.1) so answers vary as little as possible. Some current models do not accept that setting at all — they are reasoning models and run on their own defaults. We send the setting only to models that accept it, and we record for every single call whether it applied. We do not claim a setting was used when it was not.

What we look for. Two separate things.

First, is the answer right? Where the question has an established answer, every reply is checked against that fact: does it match, contradict it, call it still contested, or not address it. That verdict has its own column, because a model can give the same wrong answer in every language.

Second, is the answer the same in every language? Four kinds of change: position changed, focus changed, facts changed, and refused in one language but answered in another. Only the last three, at Level 2 or above, count as a finding.

- Level 0 — Same answer: the main meaning is the same.

- Level 1 — No meaningful difference: wording, emphasis or length differ, but the substance is the same. We do not treat this as a finding.

- Level 2 — Meaning changed: an important claim, fact, or conclusion changes.

- Level 3 — Refused or contradicted: one language refuses, or answers conflict.

Risk level comes from the highest level found: None / Low / Medium / High. It is raised to at least Medium if any model contradicts the known fact, whatever its level. Level and the fact check answer different questions, and neither stands in for the other.

Answer length. Each answer gets a normal budget first. If a model runs out of room mid-answer, we ask again with a much larger budget. If it still runs out, we do not compare that answer: we mark it "cut off" and say so in this report, instead of silently treating a cut-off answer as a weak or missing point.

Repeats. Every question is asked once per model per language to start with. Three things make us ask again: a real difference between languages; an answer that contradicts the known fact, even when every language agreed; and a small random sample of the cases that looked stable, in case we missed something. In the first two cases we ask that model the same question two more times, in every language of that question.

One answer round means asking the model once in every tested language. Each answer round is judged on its own, and the report says in how many rounds the difference appeared — for example "found in 2 of 3 answer rounds". We never write plain "stable" without that count. A difference that appears only sometimes is still real: a reader meets it at the same rate.

Cost limit. Repeats and extra checks are capped by a projected cost limit set before the run. Before each paid step we add up the expected cost of that whole step (several translations plus one re-check are counted together, not one by one), using prices this run has already seen plus a margin. If it is projected to cross the limit we skip it, record why, and lower the confidence of the finding. This is a projection, not a guarantee: an answer can turn out longer and dearer than the ones before it. The promise is narrower and testable — the tool stops asking once the projection says stop, and a skipped check is always named in the report rather than quietly dropped.

Second opinion. Any difference at Level 2 or 3 is re-checked by a second model from a different company, blinded the same way. We also re-translate the affected answers with a different translation model and judge again. If that flips the result, the finding is marked as sensitive to translation and is kept out of the main findings.

Translation check. Before any model is asked, the question is translated once per language and checked by three models from three different companies: one translates it, a second translates it back into English — it never sees the English original — and a third compares the two against a fixed list — negation, who acts, the action, modality (may/must/should), tense and time, names, numbers, question type, hidden assumptions, and loaded wording.

If wording drifted, we translate that language again, once. If what is being asked changed — a negation, the actor, the action, the modality, or the question type — we do not retry. That language is not measured, and it is listed in "Missing answers". No person is asked to check by hand. Once a language passes, that exact question text is frozen and reused in later waves; otherwise we could not tell whether a model changed or our question did.

This check finds risk. It does not prove a translation is correct. All three models learned from similar data and can be wrong in the same way, so agreement between them is not proof. Disagreement is the reliable signal.

Known facts. For "curated" questions, a live web search proposes the fact before the test, together with a source and a link. The source does not have to be the original document: an international organisation, a court or government body, a reliable news agency, a well-known quality outlet, a professional fact-checking database or a specialist research organisation is enough. We then try to open that page. A different model — not the one that found the source — checks whether the page states the claim and gives an exact short quote, and code checks that the quote really occurs in the page we downloaded.

Four outcomes. None of them hides the cross-language result:

- Source checked — the page was opened and supports the fact. Factual accuracy is rated normally.

- Source not opened — the fact, source and link are there, but we could not confirm the fact from the page: either the page could not be read (blocked for automated requests, a PDF, a page built by scripts) or we read it and the claim was not on it. Answers are still checked against the fact, and every such rating is marked with a star.

- Source conflict — the page says something incompatible with the fact. We do not decide who is right, so factual accuracy is not rated at all.

- Source missing — no usable fact and source were found. Factual accuracy is not rated.

Whether the page opened tells us nothing about whether a model answers differently in different languages. Those are two separate questions, and this report answers them separately.

**Limits**

- This audit covers one question and the models listed above.

- Answers may change when the same question is asked again.

- Translation and automated checks can miss details.
