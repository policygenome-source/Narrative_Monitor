Romania's 2024 Presidential Election Annulment

Romania's Constitutional Court took the unprecedented step of annulling the first round of the 2024 presidential election just two days before the scheduled runoff. Here's what happened:

The trigger event On 6 December, Romania's Constitutional Court annulled the outcome of the country's presidential first round elections. This came as a shock because the first round had been narrowly won by far-right ultranationalist candidate Calin Georgescu amid allegations of Russian interference. The decision meant a second-round runoff scheduled for that Sunday, between Georgescu and centrist rival Elena Lasconi, would no longer take place.

Official legal basis In a press release, the court said the annulment was based on Article 146(f) of the Constitution, emphasizing the need to ensure the correctness and legality of the electoral process. The court's decision was announced as final and binding, requiring the Romanian government to restart the electoral calendar.

The alleged Russian interference The core justification was intelligence about a coordinated online influence campaign. The court's order that the presidential electoral process must be "entirely redone" came after Romanian President Klaus Iohannis declassified intelligence reports alleging a Russian interference campaign geared toward benefiting Georgescu on TikTok and Telegram. Specifically, the secret services alleged that one TikTok user paid more than $381,000 to other users to promote Georgescu content.

A twist: an earlier recount that found nothing Notably, this wasn't the court's first intervention. Following vote rigging allegations made by a minor candidate, the Constitutional Court of Romania ordered a recount, but ultimately decided to confirm the results of the first round on 2 December. Then, just days later, on 6 December, the Constitutional Court reversed their decision and controversially annulled the first round of the election, after intelligence documents were declassified stating that Russia had run a coordinated online campaign to promote Georgescu.

Domestic analysis noted the sequence was messy: the first round upended expectations with Georgescu winning 22.9%, and controversy escalated as public authorities approved a recount requested by a fringe candidate despite the absence of any evidence of fraud — a recount that was conducted without independent observers and confirmed the initial results, which the court then validated. However, the election's annulment was triggered days later after a meeting of the Supreme Council of National Defence and a declassification of secret files.

Reactions The decision was deeply polarizing. Even Georgescu's electoral rival criticized it: Romanian society appeared divided in its reaction, with even the liberal opposition leader, Elena Lasconi, Georgescu's opponent in the presidential race, criticizing the decision. Lasconi herself said the court's decision was "illegal, immoral, and crushes the very essence of democracy."

Analysts framed this as a watershed moment for election integrity globally: while historically annulments have typically been associated with clear procedural errors or fraud, the Romanian ruling signified an important shift, with the court's decision highlighting the impact of extensive deployment of artificial intelligence, automated systems, and coordinated information integrity campaigns on electoral integrity, among other findings of interference.

Aftermath Romanian prosecutors launched criminal investigations against Georgescu for supporting fascist groups and electoral violations, while authorities rescheduled the election for May 2025. The saga triggered sustained unrest — throughout December 2024 to March 2025, pro-Georgescu and anti-government protests escalated in scale and intensity, with tens of thousands gathering in Bucharest. Ultimately, Georgescu was later barred from running in the rerun by the Central Electoral Bureau, and the eventual 2025 election was won by Nicușor Dan.

Background

On November 24, 2024, Romania held the first round of its presidential election, and it produced a stunning upset: The first round of the presidential election was held on November 24, 2024, with Georgescu receiving the most votes. Romanian voters delivered an unexpected victory to a right-wing populist named Călin Georgescu in the opening round of the country's presidential election. Always considered a longshot, Georgescu had been polling in the single digits just weeks before surging to claim first place with 23 percent of the vote. He was set to face pro-EU candidate Elena Lasconi in a runoff.

Why It Was Annulled

1. Allegations of Russian interference and a coordinated TikTok campaign

Just days before the scheduled runoff, the presidential office released intelligence documents pointing to an influence campaign that had backed Georgescu, and evidence of Russian interference and illegal financing. More specifically, Romanian President Klaus Iohannis declassified intelligence reports alleging a Russian interference campaign geared toward benefiting Georgescu on TikTok and Telegram.

The intelligence findings were quite specific: Romania's president, Klaus Iohannis, declassified five intelligence files related to the campaign investigation. One document indicated that 25,000 pro-Georgescu TikTok accounts became highly active two weeks before the first-round vote. Nearly 800 of these accounts had been created in 2016 and remained largely dormant until the election. Investigators found that the activity of these accounts was organised through a Telegram channel, which coordinated the content that was distributed on TikTok. One report also alleged that one TikTok user paid more than $381,000 to other users to promote Georgescu content.

2. Illegal campaign financing and other irregularities

Beyond the social media manipulation, the declassified documents pointed to evidence of voting manipulation through social media platforms, illegal campaign financing on TikTok, cyber-attacks orchestrated by external forces and suspected Russian interference.

3. The Constitutional Court's ruling

Based on this evidence, on December 6, 2024, Romania's constitutional court annulled the first round of the country's 2024 presidential election process, which was narrowly won by far-right ultranationalist candidate Calin Georgescu amid allegations of Russian interference. Legally, the court said the annulment was based on Article 146(f) of the Constitution, emphasizing the need to ensure the correctness and legality of the electoral process. The ruling was sweeping: the court's decision, announced Friday, is final and binding, and requires the Romanian government to restart the electoral calendar.

Context and Controversy

The decision was highly unusual because it followed an earlier ruling that had actually confirmed the results. As one analysis noted, controversy escalated as the public authorities approved a recount requested by a fringe candidate, despite the absence of any evidence of fraud, and the recount, conducted without independent observers or polling station members, confirmed the initial results, which the constitutional court validated. Then, the election's annulment was triggered days later after a meeting of the Supreme Council of National Defence and a declassification of secret files.

The ruling was historically unprecedented — Romania is the first European country to cancel an election over alleged Russian interference, although similar allegations have been levied in the past, such as in Moldova. The reaction was deeply divisive, and notably even Georgescu's opponent objected: Romanian society appears divided in its reaction, with even the liberal opposition leader, Elena Lasconi, Georgescu's opponent in the presidential race, criticizing the decision. Lasconi called the ruling "illegal, immoral, and crushes the very essence of democracy," while Georgescu described the decision to annul the first round and delay the second round as a state coup.

Critics also pointed to institutional failure: the unprecedented annulment of Romania's presidential election, decided while the diaspora was voting for the second round and just two days before polling stations were due to open domestically, showcases a critical failure of the national public authorities to ensure electoral safety and integrity. Because the annulment came so close to voting, the delayed enforcement of the election's annulment resulted in nearly 48,000 Romanians casting votes that were ultimately invalid.

Aftermath

A rerun election was held in May 2025. Georgescu himself was later barred from running in the new race, and the eventual winner was pro-European independent Nicușor Dan — the eventual victory of Nicușor Dan, a pro-European independent reformer, restored a measure of democratic legitimacy but also exposed enduring vulnerabilities in Romania's political system.

Ключевым триггером стали рассекреченные президентом Клаусом Йоханнисом документы спецслужб (Высшего совета национальной обороны, разведки и МВД). Они указывали на: - Координированную кампанию в соцсетях (особенно TikTok и Telegram): тысячи аккаунтов (включая ~800, созданных в 2016 году иностранным государством и активированных перед выборами, плюс ~25 000 новых), идентичные сообщения, инфлюенсеров и неоплаченную/недекларированную рекламу. Контент не маркировался как предвыборный, что нарушало законы. Один аккаунт выплатил сотни тысяч долларов за продвижение Джорджеску.[1] - Массовые кибератаки (более 85 000 попыток) на избирательную инфраструктуру, которые спецслужбы связали с государственным актором (Россией как «враждебным государством», ведущим гибридные атаки).[4] - Непрозрачное финансирование кампании Джорджеску и использование цифровых технологий/ИИ, что дало ему несправедливое преимущество. Румынская разведка описала это как «высокоорганизованную партизанскую» операцию, похожую на российские методы влияния.[5]

Ранее суд подтвердил результаты после пересчёта голосов (по жалобе на возможные ошибки), но новые данные изменили позицию. Премьер Марчел Чолаку назвал отмену «единственно правильным решением» из-за «вопиющего искажения» волеизъявления из-за российского вмешательства. Россия отрицала причастность.[2]

Решение было беспрецедентным и вызвало споры: сторонники Джорджеску назвали его «переворотом», а некоторые аналитики позже указывали на возможные внутренние факторы (например, расследование о роли румынской партии в TikTok-кампании). Однако официальная причина суда — обеспечение честности выборов на фоне доказательств иностранного вмешательства. Новые президентские выборы прошли в 2025 году.

How we checked

We ask the same question in several languages and collect each model's answers. Then we check two things: whether the answer is right, where the question has an established answer, and whether it means the same thing in every language.

We call each model directly through an API, not through its chat app. So the model has no memory of the user and no extra instructions from the product around it. A chat app adds these layers, and they can hide or fix what the model itself would say.

Web access. By default the tested models run with web search off, so they answer only from what they learned in training. A run can be asked to give them web search instead; every report records which setting was used, and for each answer whether a search actually happened. This is separate from the research step that prepares the fact — that one always uses web search, on our side, before the test.

Settings. We ask for a low, steady setting (temperature 0.1) so answers vary as little as possible. Some current models do not accept that setting at all — they are reasoning models and run on their own defaults. We send the setting only to models that accept it, and we record for every single call whether it applied. We do not claim a setting was used when it was not.

What we look for. Two separate things.

First, is the answer right? Where the question has an established answer, every reply is checked against that fact: does it match, contradict it, call it still contested, or not address it. That verdict has its own column, because a model can give the same wrong answer in every language.

Second, is the answer the same in every language? Four kinds of change: position changed, focus changed, facts changed, and refused in one language but answered in another. Only the last three, at Level 2 or above, count as a finding.

  • Level 0 — Same answer: the main meaning is the same.
  • Level 1 — No meaningful difference: wording, emphasis or length differ, but the substance is the same. We do not treat this as a finding.
  • Level 2 — Meaning changed: an important claim, fact, or conclusion changes.
  • Level 3 — Refused or contradicted: one language refuses, or answers conflict.

Risk level comes from the highest level found: None / Low / Medium / High. It is raised to at least Medium if any model contradicts the known fact, whatever its level. Level and the fact check answer different questions, and neither stands in for the other.

Answer length. Each answer gets a normal budget first. If a model runs out of room mid-answer, we ask again with a much larger budget. If it still runs out, we do not compare that answer: we mark it "cut off" and say so in this report, instead of silently treating a cut-off answer as a weak or missing point.

Repeats. Every question is asked once per model per language to start with. Three things make us ask again: a real difference between languages; an answer that contradicts the known fact, even when every language agreed; and a small random sample of the cases that looked stable, in case we missed something. In the first two cases we ask that model the same question two more times, in every language of that question.

One answer round means asking the model once in every tested language. Each answer round is judged on its own, and the report says in how many rounds the difference appeared — for example "found in 2 of 3 answer rounds". We never write plain "stable" without that count. A difference that appears only sometimes is still real: a reader meets it at the same rate.

Cost limit. Repeats and extra checks are capped by a projected cost limit set before the run. Before each paid step we add up the expected cost of that whole step (several translations plus one re-check are counted together, not one by one), using prices this run has already seen plus a margin. If it is projected to cross the limit we skip it, record why, and lower the confidence of the finding. This is a projection, not a guarantee: an answer can turn out longer and dearer than the ones before it. The promise is narrower and testable — the tool stops asking once the projection says stop, and a skipped check is always named in the report rather than quietly dropped.

Second opinion. Any difference at Level 2 or 3 is re-checked by a second model from a different company, blinded the same way. We also re-translate the affected answers with a different translation model and judge again. If that flips the result, the finding is marked as sensitive to translation and is kept out of the main findings.

Translation check. Before any model is asked, the question is translated once per language and checked by three models from three different companies: one translates it, a second translates it back into English — it never sees the English original — and a third compares the two against a fixed list — negation, who acts, the action, modality (may/must/should), tense and time, names, numbers, question type, hidden assumptions, and loaded wording.

If wording drifted, we translate that language again, once. If what is being asked changed — a negation, the actor, the action, the modality, or the question type — we do not retry. That language is not measured, and it is listed in "Missing answers". No person is asked to check by hand. Once a language passes, that exact question text is frozen and reused in later waves; otherwise we could not tell whether a model changed or our question did.

This check finds risk. It does not prove a translation is correct. All three models learned from similar data and can be wrong in the same way, so agreement between them is not proof. Disagreement is the reliable signal.

Known facts. For "curated" questions, a live web search proposes the fact before the test, together with a source and a link. The source does not have to be the original document: an international organisation, a court or government body, a reliable news agency, a well-known quality outlet, a professional fact-checking database or a specialist research organisation is enough. We then try to open that page. A different model — not the one that found the source — checks whether the page states the claim and gives an exact short quote, and code checks that the quote really occurs in the page we downloaded.

Four outcomes. None of them hides the cross-language result:

  • Source checked — the page was opened and supports the fact. Factual accuracy is rated normally.
  • Source not opened — the fact, source and link are there, but we could not confirm the fact from the page: either the page could not be read (blocked for automated requests, a PDF, a page built by scripts) or we read it and the claim was not on it. Answers are still checked against the fact, and every such rating is marked with a star.
  • Source conflict — the page says something incompatible with the fact. We do not decide who is right, so factual accuracy is not rated at all.
  • Source missing — no usable fact and source were found. Factual accuracy is not rated.

Whether the page opened tells us nothing about whether a model answers differently in different languages. Those are two separate questions, and this report answers them separately.

Limits

  • This audit covers one question and the models listed above.
  • Answers may change when the same question is asked again.
  • Translation and automated checks can miss details.
